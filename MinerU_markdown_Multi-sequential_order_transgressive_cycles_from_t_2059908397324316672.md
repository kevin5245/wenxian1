# Abstract

High-resolution sequence analysis of three drill-cores with well-constrained optically simulated luminescence ages reveals a stratigraphic evolution of the Baeksu tidal flat (south-west coast of Korea) that documents the development of tidedominated estuary infill interacting with minimal fluvial processes since marine isotope stage 6. The stratigraphic architecture and its correlation along an upper mudflat-to-subtidal transect showcase two different hierarchies of sequences and small-scale (fifth-order) subsequences that are nested within larger-scale (fourthorder) sequences. Two fourth-order sequences with a frequency of 100 kyr consist of lower fluvial deposits and overlying tidal deposits, respectively, reflecting a twofold regressive–transgressive sedimentary sequence related to glacial–interglacial cycles. The sequence boundary is marked by an abrupt facies change from oxidised spit gravels (marine isotope stage 3) to tidal muds (marine isotope stage 1). An interesting feature is the presence of two packages of retrograding spit/tidal deposits corresponding to marine isotope stage 5a/b and marine isotope stage 3 interstadials deposited during short-lived sea-level rise, in spite of the long-term phase of glacial sea-level fall spanning from marine isotope stage 5d to marine isotope stage 2. Identification of deeply oxidised tidal deposits dated to marine isotope stage 5a/b confirms that two short-term fluctuations in sea level occurred during the long-lived glacial period. Such nested successions are assigned as fifthorder subsequences with 40 kyr time intervals. The late Quaternary Baeksu tidal flat succession was thus mainly controlled by two different frequencies and magnitudes of sea-level changes, associated with major interglacials (marine isotope stage 5e and 1) and minor interstadials (marine isotope stage 5a and 3). More importantly, transgressive episodes are significantly better preserved than regressive phases. This study provides a good example of how multi-order, multiple transgressive deposition may be preserved in a tide-dominated estuarine setting, particularly where river inputs are negligible.

# 1 | INTRODUCTION

The interpretation of ancient or modern sedimentary deposits requires knowledge of two discrete but interconnected techniques on the basis of which sedimentary successions can be identified. Facies analysis is the first step to correctly interpret the diagnostic depositional environment (Walker & James,  1992; Posamentier & Walker, 2006; James & Dalrymple, 2010). In the next step, the stratigraphic successions are classified into genetically related sequences within the chronological framework (Catuneanu, 2006, 2019). The integration of the two techniques or tools enables a more realistic reconstruction of the palaeogeography and evolutionary patterns of depositional facies that vary in space and time. Sequencestratigraphic analysis thus helps to identify sequential changes in the nature of facies or depositional units and the translation of facies belts, namely a sequence of cycles driven by changes in accommodation. 

The fluvial-marine transition zones in estuaries and deltas are very dynamic and complicated depositional environments because fluvial and marine processes interact there and lead to an inherently complex infill history (Dalrymple & Choi,  2007; Amorosi et  al.,  2016). Transgressive-to-regressive turnaround in the coastal zone occurs cyclically, resulting in the translation of facies tracts from proximal-to-distal and vice versa. As transgression proceeds, the shoreline migrates landwards, being generally accompanied by a simple landward translation of facies belts, which results in estuarine infilling. The opposite occurs in deltaic deposition when regressive phases ensue (Cattaneo & Steel,  2003; Catnueanu,  2019). The signatures of such transgressive-regressive cycles are best preserved in coastal environments. However, the reconstruction of the infill history of estuaries and deltas often poses serious problems because transgressive-regressive turnarounds are not simply regular with respect to the generalised model proposed by Zaitlin et al. (1994). This is mainly due to the inherent depositional complexity, particularly in tide-dominated estuarine and deltaic systems. 

Along the west coast of the Korean peninsula, extensive macrotidal flats exhibit late Quaternary sedimentary infills characterised by two typical estuarine successions that are unconformably separated by highly oxidised mud layers and yellowish coarse-grained gravel beds, predominantly deposited in fluvial environments and which underwent long subaerial exposure during successive sea-level lowstands (Choi & Dalrymple,  2004; Choi & Kim,  2006; Chang et  al.,  2014; Baek et  al.,  2017; Yoon et al., 2023). The latter were interpreted as having been deposited in fluvially-dominated channels and interfluves. Accordingly, the late Quaternary tidal deposits along the Korean coastal zone are considered to have formed in the course of at least two transgression-regression cycles. Recent investigations, however, have questioned such a well-balanced record of transgressive-to-regressive deposition in this highly-complex tidally-dominated estuarine setting (Lim et al., 2022, 2023). Here, the late Quaternary tidal successions are revisited by analysing two additional drill-cores (core C01 and C02), in combination with previously published data of core C04 from Chang et al. (2014) obtained on the Baeksu tidal flats located on the southwest coast of Korea. 

For this purpose, a detailed facies analysis of the three deep-drilled sediment cores collected along a proximalto-distal transect across the tidal flat was carried out. The numerous identified facies were thereafter grouped into facies associations (FAs) of diagnostic character. Sequence-stratigraphic analysis was successfully performed based on Wheeler's chronostratigraphy diagram, supported by numerous optically simulated luminescence (OSL) age datings (Wheeler, 1958; Qayyum et al., 2015). Accordingly, the study primarily aims at documenting the sedimentary facies, facies-tract architectures, sequence organisation and their late Quaternary stratigraphic evolution within a proximal-to-distal tide-dominated estuarine framework. In addition, the common bias in the interpretation of estuary infills is addressed and, more importantly, the existence of cryptic bounding surfaces, which are not detectable without closely spaced age dates, are highlighted, these being at the root of erroneous identifications and interpretations of supposed sequences and their bounding surfaces in the rock record. 

# 2 | PHYSICAL SETTING OF THE STUDY AREA

The Korean peninsula is characterised by major sedimentary basins and orogenic belts that were actively formed from the late Proterozoic to Neogene (Chough et al., 2000) (Figure 1). Compared to the eastern coast of the Korean peninsula, the western part has a lower relief and has been in a more tectonically stable condition since the Miocene (Park et al., 1997). Thus, the relative sea-level fluctuations during the late Pleistocene on the west coast of Korea are mainly attributed to glacio-eustatic changes. The Baeksu macrotidal flat, located on the south-west coast of Korea, directly accesses the open ocean without any barriers in front, and is therefore strongly influenced by seasonal waves, particularly in winter (Figure 1). The tidal flat is up to 8.5 km wide and 6 km long at low tide. It has a very lowgradient slope, about $0 . 0 6 ^ { \circ }$ on average and is characteristically featureless with no major tidal channels except for subtle undulations, which are interpreted to be intertidal swash bars (Yang et al., 2005, 2006). The rocky headlands, situated in the outer zone, consist mainly of Jurassic granite and Cretaceous andesitic tuff. The Chilsan-do archipelago consists of seven islands located about 7 km offshore from the modern shoreline. Small, shallow streams frame the tidal flats on either side (Figure 2A), but rapidly diminish in the offshore direction. No major river systems are developed in the area, and river inputs are therefore negligible. Historical maps show that almost half of the estuarine tidal flats have been reclaimed for agricultural purposes since the 1930s, today comprising salt pans, shrimp farms and rice fields. Prior to reclamation, a more than 10 km long tidal flat with a prominent funnel-shaped embayment was developed. The original shoreline is located about 3 km landward from the modern diked shoreline, which is characterised by coastal dune fields. Some aeolian dunes overlie the granite basement of Jurassic age (Figure 2B).

The tides in the area are semi-diurnal with a mean tidal range of 3.9 m, corresponding to the lower macrotidal regime (Hayes, 1979; Flemming, 2005). Maximum tidal current speeds on the intertidal flats reach $4 0 \mathrm { c m s ^ { - 1 } }$ at spring tides and $2 5 \mathrm { c m s } ^ { - 1 }$ at neap tides (Baek et  al.,  2017). The wind and waves are strongly seasonal, with generally calm conditions in summer and frequent intense storms in winter. The strong seasonality of the wind-generated waves has led to contrasting sedimentation patterns, for example, thick mud deposition in summer and wave-dominated sand flats in winter (Figure 2C–F; Yang et al., 2005). The reader is referred to Yang et al. (2005) for more detail on the monsoon wave climate and resulting seasonal sedimentation patterns in the area.

# 3 | DATASET AND METHODOLOGY

To determine the facies development and stratigraphy of the late Quaternary tidal deposits, three long sediment cores were collected along a proximal–distal transect, crossing the Baeksu tidal flat on the south-western coast of Korea (Figure 1). Coring was performed using a hydraulic-powered drilling system that is capable of obtaining undisturbed sediment samples. Core C04 (2.2 m below mean sea level), about 46 m long, was taken at the lower, distal intertidal zone about 5.5 km seaward from the original shoreline (Chang et al., 2014). Core C01 (2.8 m above mean sea level), about 21 m long, was retrieved in the mid-intertidal area close to the present seawall, about 2.7 km eastward from the first core and about 2.9 km seaward from the coastal dune fields. Core C02 (3.0 m above mean sea level), about 25 m long, was retrieved almost at the original coastline, being located in the upper intertidal zone. All of the cores penetrated down to the buried basement consisting of Jurassic granite. 

The collected core sections were initially kept in low-temperature storage until they were split lengthwise into two halves in the laboratory. One half was photographed and described in terms of sedimentary structures, colours, mean grain size, textures and bioturbation index (BI) before being archived. Sediment colour was measured by comparison with the Munsell colour chart. The BI was documented by following the procedure proposed by Taylor and Goldring (1993). The other half of the core was used to obtain resin peels for a detailed documentation of the primary physical sedimentary structures. 

The chronological framework is based on a total of 40 OSL dates. The type of detailed chronological information and methodological techniques for OSL dating are described in Kim et al. (2015). The OSL dating of all 40 samples was conducted at the Korea Institute of Geoscience and Mineral Resources, Daejeon, using standard techniques (Table 1; Murray & Wintle, 2000). Among them, 18 OSL ages of the C04 core were extracted from Chang et al. (2014).

# 4.2.2 | Interpretation

The about 9 m thick mud deposits with silt streaks and plant remains (in some places displaying yellowish spots) indicate deposition in a high-lying mudflat/saltmarsh environment influenced by repeated low-energy tidal processes. Active deposition in that setting occurs around high-water slack tides, the area being prone to subaerial exposure during the rest of the time. The presence of sand facies, for example, sand patches, massive to laminated sand-mud representing upper flow regime conditions, indicates sporadic inundation during storm events (Reineck & Gerdes, 1996; Chang et al., 2006, 2014). Moreover, the graded gSM and gxgM facies with fining-upward grainsize trends ending in homogenous or inclined stratification incorporating organic-rich mud or plant-debris are interpreted to represent tide-dominated point bar and saltmarsh environments formed by laterally shifting channels (Cosma et al., 2021, 2022). Unlike other associations, the deeply oxidised, bioturbated lenticular bedding (facies blMo) served as a marker for separating MIS 3 and MIS 5a/b deposits, thus suggesting long periods of subaerial exposure after deposition during sea-level lowstands. However, the slightly oxidised mMo facies is interpreted to have formed in a saltmarsh environment prone to aerial exposure. In accordance with OSL dates, the FA2 saltmarsh/mudflat facies association was deposited under normal low-energy conditions near the high-tide level during MIS 5e, MIS 5a/b, MIS 3 and currently MIS 1, corresponding to the early to late Holocene. 

# 4.3 | Facies association 3 (FA3): Mixed flat

# 4.3.1 | Description

Facies association 3 is found in the lower and upper parts of core C04, being particularly thick (18.5m) in the lower succession. This association occupies only the lower part in core C01 with a thickness of 1.1m, but is not observed in core C02 (Figures 3–5). The association consists of sandmud alternations (facies pSM, pSMsp, pSMd) with interbedded gravels (facies mG) and laminated silty sand/sand (facies lzS/lS). Sand-mud alternations, the major component of core FA3, are characterised by repeated thin sand and mud laminae with systematic changes in their thickness (Figure 6E). Peats are included in some places. Laminae are partly deformed, especially in the nearby facies mG, which consists of 7cm thick sub-rounded granules. Bioturbation is low to intense (BI=2–5), and is relatively more abundant in core C04 than in core C01. The OSL dates range from $1 0 9 . 9 7 { \scriptstyle \pm 6 . 6 4 }$ to 138.78±7.84ka in the lower succession. 

# 4.3.2 | Interpretation

Rhythmically alternating sand-mud couplets indicate tidal bedding in a mixed tidal flat (Dalrymple et al., 1991; Chang et  al.,  2006, 2014). Interbedded gravels are considered to have been deposited by flooding. These conspicuous facies in FA3 are commonly regarded as tidal rhythmites. For this reason, FA3 is interpreted as a mixed tidal flat deposit under increasing tidal strength, rather than as a saltmarsh/mudflat deposit. The OSL dates indicate deposition during the MIS 5e sea-level highstand. Although OSL dating was not performed for the upper part of core C04, it seems to have been formed during the late Holocene transgression, correlating with FA2 in the upper succession of core C01. 

# 4.4 | Facies association 4 (FA4): Sand flat

# 4.4.1 | Description

This FA is only found in the upper part of core C02. It comprises a 3.2 m thick sand-dominated facies composed of wavy-bedded sand and mud couplets (facies wSM), small-scale cross-laminated sand (facies lS), massive sand (facies mS) and liquefied sand (facies qS) (Figure 3). The sediment generally consists of well-sorted, medium to fine sands. The mud facies grades upward from facies mS containing mud chips and scattered shell fragments into facies wSM, lS and qS, which are characterised by highly undulating couplets, small-scale trough crossbeds with mud drapes and frequent disturbance by liquefaction, which occurs in sands when sediment strength is temporally reduced by increased pore fluid pressure through rapid sediment accumulation (Figure 6F). Bioturbation is sparse with a low diversity, having a BI of 1. Facies association FA4 is dated to $0 . 9 0 { \pm } 0 . 0 4 \mathrm { k a }$ . 

# 4.4.2 | Interpretation

Cross-laminated sand containing mud lenses is interpreted as flaser bedding. It is formed by migrating dunes or ripples at maximum current speed in the middle of a tidal cycle, being subsequently draped by thin mud layers at slack tide under relatively low-energy conditions (Reineck & Singh,  1980; Dalrymple,  2010). Additionally, Facies mS and wSM, which contain mud chips and shell fragments, are suggestive of sand flat environments that were occasionally exposed to waves during the late Holocene transgression. 

# 4.5 | Facies association 5 (FA5): Swash bar and trough on upper intertidal flat

# 4.5.1 | Description

Facies association 5 occurs only in the upper portions of cores C01 and C04. Its thickness varies from 2.0 to 5.2m, core C01 generally displaying greater thicknesses (Figures 4 and 5). This succession can be generally divided into a sandy and a muddy facies. The sandy facies consists of massive sand (facies mS) and parallel to cross-laminated sand (facies pS/xS). Notably, this sandy facies, which is up to 2 m thick, shows bidirectional cross-laminations that are in contact with underlying and overlying muddy facies. It displays both sharp and gradational contact features. The muddy facies are characterised by laminated and semiconsolidated silty sand (facies lzS/czS), sand-mud alternations (facies pSM, pSMd) and massive to liquefied mud (facies mM/qM). Such sand/mud repetitions are a typical feature of FA5 (Figure 6G). The degree of bioturbation is key to distinguishing between sand and mud facies, being hence dominant in the mud facies (BI=3) but, in contrast, is not observed in the sand facies (BI=0). The OSL ages range from 0.42±0.06 to 1.94±0.2ka. 

# 4.5.2 | Interpretation

Nonbioturbated parallel to cross-laminated sand beds result from strong swash and backwash processes. The presence of bidirectional cross-lamination between the uppermost and lowermost parts of individual sand beds indicates lateral amalgamation of migrating sand bars. Additionally, evidence of swash bar migration/welding processes is also manifested by sharp contacts with mud deposits (e.g. semi-consolidated mud) accumulated in protected bar troughs. These, however, become exposed when bars migrate landward during storms (Yang et al., 2005, 2021; Yang & Chang, 2018). Considering that burrows mostly occur in bar troughs, and that the sandmud alternations are formed by tidal processes. Facies FA5 is interpreted to represent swash bars on upper intertidal flats formed during the late Holocene transgression. In fact, some swash bars are currently observed to develop on the upper tidal flat of the Baeksu area (Figure 2E). 

# 4.6 | Facies association 6 (FA6): Intertidal shoreface

# 4.6.1 | Description

Facies association 6 occupies the upper succession of cores C01 and C04 and conformably overlies FA5 (Figures 4 and 5). This association consists of massive sand with mud chips (facies mS), bioturbated sand (facies bS), hummocky to cross-bedded sand (facies hxS/xS), laminated silty sand (facies lzS), wave-rippled muddy sand (facies xmS), sandmud couplets (facies pSM), shelly gravelly sand bed (facies mgSsh) and massive mud (facies mM). Generally, it is characterised by wave-dominated facies, especially in core C04, including long-wavelength hummocky crossstratification (HCS, hereafter) and interbedded shell beds (Figure 6H). Bioturbation intensity is overall low, ranging from absent to sparse (BI = 0–1), except for facies bS of the uppermost part of core C01 where the BI is 3. The OSL dated ages of FA6 range from 0.21 ± 0.04 to 0.55 ± 0.03 ka. 

# 4.6.2 | Interpretation

Sedimentary features of FA6 differ depending on whether the core is located more offshore (e.g. core C04) or more landward (e.g. core C01). Wave-formed sedimentary structures, such as HCS-like cross-bedding with long wavelengths and intercalated shelly beds, are interpreted as storm deposits occurring farther offshore (core C04), whereas in the landward core, shorter wavelength HCSs and abundant mud layers or chips are recognised (core C01). Such landward decreases in both HCS and thickness of storm beds are probably to result from progressive dissipation of wave energy due to bottom friction (Dashtgard et al., 2021; Yang et al., 2005, 2006, 2021). Based on this evidence, FA6 is interpreted as an intertidal shoreface, where wave-formed structures (e.g. HCSs and storm beds) are generated during storms, followed by tidal rhythmites during calm conditions (Chang & Flemming, 2006). Facies association 6 was formed during the late Holocene transgression. 

# 4.7 | Facies association 7 (FA7): Sand beach

# 4.7.1 | Description

The about 30 cm thick FA7 is only found in the upper part of core C02. This relatively thin association is characterised by massive sand (facies mS), cross-laminated sand (facies xS) and parallel interbedded mud (facies pM) (Figure 3). The sand facies display well-sorted, fine to medium sands that consist predominantly of quartz and feldspar, and even contain mud clasts or layered mud beds. It shows a fining-upward grain-size trend and has an unconformable erosive contact with the underlying FA8. Dark-coloured to light-coloured thin layers alternate, showing low-angle cross-laminations. The colour of the beds ranges from very dark grey (10YR 3/1) to pale yellow (5Y 7/4) (Figure 6I). Shell fragments are often present, but no bioturbation is visible (BI = 0). The OSL age of FA7 is $1 . 0 9 \pm 0 . 1 1$ ka. 

# 4.7.2 | Interpretation

Dark–light alternating laminae are composed of heavy minerals and felsic minerals, respectively. Low-angle cross-laminations of the quartz-dominated sand facies are indicative of sandy beach deposits that represent highenergy swash action along the beachface. As ocean waves approach the beach, water particles change their form from circular to elliptical and finally to horizontal to-andfro motions (Komar,  1998; Boggs,  2006; Clifton,  2006; Yang & Chang,  2018). Facies association 7 was formed during the late Holocene transgression. 

# 4.8 | Facies association 8 (FA8): Spit and Back-spit

# 4.8.1 | Description

The 1.2–7.2 m thick FA8 is olive grey (5Y 2/2) to yellowish brown (10YR 5/6) in colour, occurs in the central section of all cores (Figures 3–5), and is characterised by structureless coarser-grained material mainly composed of massive to graded gravel (facies mG/gG), massive sandy gravel (facies msG), massive to graded muddy gravel (facies mmG/gmG), graded muddy sand (facies gmS), graded gravelly mud (facies ggM) and graded sandy mud (facies gsM). Sparse cross-laminated sands (facies xS) and parallel interbedded mud layers (facies pM) are observed. Coarser sediments are mostly composed of sub-angular coarse sand to medium pebbles of variable mineral composition, but sandy gravels are predominant. Notably, some gravel beds are combined with mud and are hence poorly sorted (Figure 6J). These muddy gravel features usually occur near facies pM, which are interspersed within these gravel beds in up to 2 cm thick layers. Bioturbation intensity is overall low (BI = 0–2). If present, it occurs only in facies gsM of core C02 where it is commonly bioturbated by worms or crabs, generating structures associated with the Skolithos and Thalassinoides types. In addition, Conichnus is manifested as cone-shaped hollows formed by sea anemones, a suspension feeder. This association occurs in two chronological units corresponding to the period between 84.6 ± 5.2 ka and $9 4 . 1 \pm 5 . 7 $ ka in the lower unit, and $4 0 . 2 4 \pm 3 . 6 8$ to $6 4 . 1 \pm 5 . 2$ ka in the upper unit. In the lower unit, it is capped by FA2. In the upper unit, it is in contact with the underlying oxidised FA2 or FA3, and with the overlying FA2 or FA7 with an unconformable bounding surface. 

# 4.8.2 | Interpretation

The yellowish, poorly sorted coarser-grained nature of FA 8, together with high clast angularity, variable composition and blended with muds, indicates that the association probably represents a gravelly spit and back-spit composed of material originating from nearby exposed bedrock (Chang et  al.,  1999; Lim et  al.,  2002). The immature appearance suggests that it was transported over short distances. It is normally structureless, but visible cross-laminations are indicative of lateral spit progradation driven by longshore currents. The gravels were transported up onto the stoss side of the bar, before avalanching down the lee side to finally accumulate in the muddy back-bar trough. Hence, the muddy gravels with their interbedded mud layers are interpreted to represent a gravel bar resting on top of a mud deposit in protected troughs or sinks. It results in mud-infilling of the spaces between the gravel clasts (Dashtgard et al., 2006). In contrast, the mudfree gravel beds are interpreted as bar crests. Trace fossils such as Skolithos, Thalassinoides and Conichnus are of importance in such shallow-marine environments, being indicative of back-spit environments under relatively low-energy conditions. Considering the unconformable contact with the underlying tidal deposits (FA2 and FA3) and the age dates, the gravelly spit and back-spit of FA8 formed during MIS 5a/b and MIS 3, respectively.

# 5.1 | Fourth-order sequential cyclicity

The Baeksu tidal deposits consist of two major fourthorder sequences, a lower S1 and an upper S2 sequence, representing a temporal cycle of about 100 kyr. The bounding surfaces of these sequences are defined as an unconformity associated with subaerial oxidised gravel beds, which separate the Holocene and the late Pleistocene succession. 

The S1 includes four depositional units (Unit I, II, III and IV) assigned to the late Pleistocene. Occupying the lowermost position, Unit I comprises fluvial channel deposits (FA1) directly overlying the basement. The sequence boundary (SB) marking the lower boundary of the lowstand systems tract (LST) is clearly identified by the basal surface of Unit I, representing a subaerial unconformity caused by fluvial incision or bypass. Lateral facies variations within the fluvial channel successions show a transition from basal gravel lag to amalgamated fluvial channel sand bodies; the latter is composed of stacked fining-upward sands with a sharp and erosional base. Considering the progradational stacking pattern and unconformable contact with overlying Unit II (MIS 5e), Unit I is interpreted as the LST formed during the MIS 6

Such retrograding estuarine mud deposits constitute the TST, being formed during subsequent MIS 5e transgression. The transgressive surface (TS) is marked by an abrupt lithofacies change from fluvial sands to tidal muds, representing erosional contacts between Unit I and Unit II. It indicates a significant shoreline shift from normal regression to transgression accompanied by the transition progradation to retrogradation. The positioning of the maximum flooding surface (MFS) in S1 is difficult to pinpoint due to the absence of a normal regressive phase (e.g. prograding deltaic deposits) between Unit II and Unit III. 

In supply-limited settings, such as the Baeksu tidal flat, where the MFS exhibits a nonerosional surface, sporadically scattered yellowish plant-debris within greyish homogeneous mud is interpreted as evidence of maximum marine transgression (shown in C02 located in a relatively landward position). Since the entire layer is not fully oxidised, it is interpreted to have formed in a highlying vegetated coastal ecosystem (i.e. saltmarsh), rather than oxidation through lowering eustatic sea levels. In a seaward position (core C01, C04), however, a sharp and closely spaced unconformity separating lower mixed flat (FA3) from overlying saltmarsh/spit (FA8) is considered an extension of the MFS in the landward core. Any faintly overlying prograding phase might be eroded during a subsequent short transgressive episode (MIS 5a/b). Above the MFS, Units III and IV are composed of alternating fine-grained (FA2, saltmarsh/mudflat) and coarse-grained (FA8, spit) deposits, corresponding to MIS 5a/b and MIS 3 interstadials, respectively. These units constitute the falling stage systems tract (FSST). 

The S2, representing the Holocene sequence, consists of Unit V that can be subdivided into early Holocene tidal (Unit V(L)) and late Holocene tidal/coastal to shallow marine (Unit V(M), V(U)) deposits. The Last Glacial Maximum Unconformity (LGMU) is easily identified by an abrupt facies change from MIS 3 spit gravels (FA8) to directly overlying MIS 1 tidal muds (FA2) or beach sands (FA7). It is thus regarded as another SB representing a significant time break, but is difficult to recognise in core C01 due to the absence of visible oxidised layers or lithofacies contrast (Figure 8). Commencing with TS, the Holocene estuarine system shows a coarsening upward succession, starting from saltmarsh (Unit V(L)) to mudflat/mixed flat (Unit V(M)) and finally to intertidal/swash bar/sandflat/ beach (Unit V(M)) deposits. This reflects a retrogradational (TST) stacking pattern during the Holocene transgression. At the end of the transgression, the weakly developed highstand systems tract (HST) is identified by recurring tidal muds (FA2), representing a seaward shift of facies within the tidal system, rather than by a prograding deltaic system. The stillstand deposits might be poorly developed due to limited river input in the study area. 

# 5.2 | Fifth-order sequential cyclicity

The FSST in the uppermost part of fourth-order S1 was deposited between 94 ka and 40 ka. Notably, this period of sea-level falling is observed as two spit/estuarine packages (Unit III and IV) linked to MIS 5a/b and MIS 3 interstadials. It confirms that two episodes, belonging to a higher order, are recorded within the fifth-order sequence, which exhibits a temporal cyclicity of 40 kyr. However, the upward shallowing succession of facies bounded by the marine flooding surface is much less significant (Figures 7 and 8). Therefore, these are interpreted as subsequences (lower ss1 and upper ss2), rather than parasequences. 

Short-term transgression during MIS 5a/b and MIS 3 interstadials led to the deposition of alternating coarsegrained (spit) and fine-grained (saltmarsh/mudflat) deposits. Each unit exhibits a retrogradational stacking pattern of fifth-order TST, forming a subsequence. The TS, marking the lower boundary of TSTs, is generally represented by a gradual transition between saltmarsh/mudflat (FA2) or by an erosional surface separating lower saltmarsh muds from overlying spit gravels (FA8). The MFS, which marks the inversion from retrogradational to progradational facies architecture, is less significant owing to the absence of regressive phases. Instead of regressive deposits, intensively bioturbated tidal bedding remains highly oxidised in the uppermost part of Unit III, indicating prolonged subaerial exposure during the MIS 4 sea-level fall. Such unconformity ensures the accurate positioning of the subsequence boundary (SSB) of ss2, which is well presented in a relatively landward location (core C02). In contrast, cores C01 and C04 show a time gap without an erosional surface between the subsequences, probably due to erosion during the upcoming abrupt transgression in MIS 3. Thus, the bounding surface between subsequences not only represents a TS but also an SSB. 

# 6 | DISCUSSION

# 6.1 | Application of stratigraphic marker to the rock record

Recognised as a key surface, palaeosols formed during MIS 2 have been widely documented along the eastern margin of the Yellow Sea (west coast of Korea) (Kim & Park,  1988; Park et  al.,  1991, 1999; Choi & Park,  2000; Choi & Kim, 2006; Lim & Park, 2003; Chang et al., 2014; Baek et  al.,  2017). Widely traceable, semi-consolidated, oxidised layers with high shear strengths and low water contents, due to subaerial exposure and weathering during the LGM, have been regarded as a sequence boundary clearly separating the Holocene from underlying pre-Holocene successions (Lim & Park,  2003; Choi & Kim, 2006). According to the seismic profiles validated by borehole cores in the Yellow Sea shelf and Jeju Strait, such an interfluve sequence boundary commonly correlates laterally to up to 30 m thick coeval scattered channel-belt sand body and deltaic wedge, preserved in the outer continental shelf. It is also recognised as a lower boundary of lowstand deposits (Lee et al., 2014, 2015, 2022). 

This key surface also corresponds to fourth-order SB developed at the MIS 3–MIS 2 transition in the Baeksu area, which is well-displayed by a subaerial unconformity in cores C02 and C04. However, particularly in core C01 located in the middle intertidal zone, lateral tracing of fourth-order SB becomes increasingly hard to define unless constrained by depositional ages. This is because there are no significant changes in lithology, BI index or oxidation signatures between the Holocene (sequence S2) and the pre-Holocene (sequence S1) successions. Furthermore, in comparison to the fourth-order (lowerfrequency) sequence displaying well-marked SB, the fifth sequences (higher-frequency) are commonly limited to TS or MFS. This implies that sea-level fluctuation seems to be an important factor for the formation and preservation of the hierarchical organisation of stratigraphic bounding surfaces. During sea-level lowering, higher-frequency sealevel fluctuations can create some sequence elements, but these are often eroded or reworked by subsequent lowerfrequency sea-level changes (Strasser et  al.,  1999). As a result, higher-frequency bounding surfaces are easily overprinted by lower-frequency ones, except for a topographic high area (SSB between −11.4 and − 11.9 m in core C02). In this regard, such intangible features of bounding surfaces, which can only be delineated by closely spaced age dates, seem to allow erroneous stratigraphic interpretations, particularly in studies of ancient rock records commonly based on lithostratigraphy. Therefore, care must be taken not to overlook cryptic bounding surfaces invisible in high-frequency sequences in the outcrops. 

# 6.2 | Reappraisal of the MIS 3 and MIS 5a/b gravel beds

As seen in Figure 7, two gravel beds capped by tidal deposits occur widely in the study area, being assigned hereafter as the upper and lower gravel bed, each corresponding to MIS 3 or MIS 5a/b, and possibly MIS 6 in age. They are distinguished by their sedimentary textures. The upper gravel bed comprises poorly sorted gravelly sands mixed with muddy materials, and their particles are characterised by high angularity. The lower bed, by contrast, is composed of medium sands to gravels without any mud components, thereby displaying well-sorted clean, clastsupported sands. Contrasting textural properties of the two gravel beds may reflect differences in gravel mobility inferred from the maturity index. Therefore, the upper gravel bed is interpreted to have been deposited within a limited transport distance, not far from the source area. Moreover, the upper gravel bed exhibits low bioturbation intensity (BI = 2), commonly being associated with crab burrows such as Skolithos, Thalassinoides and Conichnus displaying cone-shaped hollows presumably constructed by sea anemones, which are known to be suspension feeders (Yang et al., 2021). On the basis of new observations including immature features, partly observed crosslaminations, and marine infauna burrows, the previous interpretation of a fluvial origin reflecting high-mobility along channel beds as postulated by Chang et al. (2014) is challenged. Thus, it is suggested here that the upper gravel bed represents marine spit deposits originating from nearby exposed bedrock vulnerable to weathering. The newborn coarser sands and gravels may have been transported alongshore to be finally deposited on the mudflat near the high-tide level. 

As a modern analogue, spit systems displaying the same textural immaturity are observed on the upper tidal flat of the southern Hampyeong Bay located about 28km away from the Baeksu area (Figure 10A). In the semi-enclosed Hampyeong Bay, surface sediments of the upper tidal flat consist mainly of gravelly muds or sandy muds with bimodal size distributions, poor sorting and high clast angularity. Mud contents tend to increase towards the land (Chang et  al.,  1999). The coarse-grained sediments are supplied here from the erosion of sea cliffs comprising weathered granitic gneiss. Furthermore, sediment supply rates are promoted by north-northwesterly winds in winter, which generate high waves that favour erosion (Chang et al., 1999; Lim et al., 2002; Hong et al., 2017). Newborn sediments eroded from basement rock thus feed the elongate spits, high-tide beaches and intertidal shoals above the upper intertidal flats (Figure 10B,C), with crab burrows frequently observed within the modern spits (Figure 10D). 

This newly adopted interpretation of the upper gravel beds as marine spits brings about challenging perspectives in redefining the sequence stratigraphy of the late Quaternary tidal deposits along the west coast of Korea. Thus, two small-scale subsequences, namely ss1 and ss2, are here newly recognised within the late Pleistocene lower sequence, assigned as the larger-scale S1 (Figure 7). Given the age validity and the tectonic stability of the west coast of Korea during the Quaternary, two small-scale sea-level highstands are proposed during the long-lasting glacial period (Figure 9). This contradicts the previous hypothesis of regressive records prior to the postglacial transgression. 

# 6.3 | Preservation potential and disparity between transgressive and regressive sedimentation

This study highlights that the MIS 6–MIS 1 succession, accurately depicted in terms of facies analysis with strong chronologic constraints, displays a temporally fourfold transgressive-dominated sedimentation, assigned to major interglacial (MIS 5e, 1) and minor interstadial (MIS 5a/b, 3) transgressive episodes. It is surprising that regressive deposits are rarely preserved, being confined to the uppermost HST (late Holocene) and basal LST (penultimate glacial maximum) or clearly marked by an oxidised layer reflecting a subaerial unconformity surface during FSST. The scarcity of regressive deposits is ascribed to the absence of major rivers in this area, which would be capable of supplying the large quantities of sediment required for their formation. 

On the other hand, the late Quaternary succession of the Po Plain–Adriatic Sea system exhibits a more complete MIS 6–MIS 1 sequence, probably led by substantial sediment flux and a high subsidence rate (ca 1 mm/yr)

Early Holocene transgressive deposits are absent at −3.8 m in the C02 core because the accommodation space was already filled with the underlying spits. 

# 7 | CONCLUSIONS

Detailed facies analysis with well-constrained OSL dating for three drill-cores has been conducted to elucidate how transgressive deposition occurs predominantly along the macrotidal shore of the Baeksu tidal flat in response to multi-frequency sea-level fluctuation since the last interglacial. This study provides key insights into the stratigraphic evolution of a tide-dominated estuary devoid of significant river inputs. 

• Five distinct sedimentary units were recognised: (i) MIS 6 basal fluvial gravel deposits, (ii) MIS 5e tidal flat deposits, (iii) MIS 5a/b spit/tidal deposits, (iv) MIS 3 spit/tidal deposits and (v) Early to late Holocene tidal/ swash bar/beach/intertidal shoreface deposits. Notably, Unit III and IV, alternating spit gravels and tidal muds, shows the retrograding stacking patterns formed during transgression. 

• In the middle of the cores, gravel beds, previously interpreted as of fluvial origin, are here reconsidered as marine spits. Newborn coarse-grained sediments may have been supplied from a nearby sea-cliff vulnerable to weathering. Gravelly spit systems currently occurring on the upper tidal flat of Hampyeong Bay are proposed as a modern analogue. 

• The stratigraphic architecture of the Baeksu tidal succession, supported by good age dates, defines two distinct different hierarchies of sequences that display smaller-scale (lower rank) subsequences nested within larger-scale (higher rank) sequences. Two fourth-order sequences, regarded as a pre-Holocene sequence (S1) and a Holocene sequence (S2), are generally composed of fluvial deposits and overlying tidal deposits (fluvial deposits not being preserved in S2) representing regressive-transgressive cycles deposited over the last two glacial–interglacial cycles. These major sequences, with a time span of 100 kyr, are clearly separated by oxidised gravel layers. The general phase of glacial sealevel fall is recorded as two packages of retrograding spit/tidal deposits, each of them being deposited during MIS 5a/b and MIS 3, and representing interstadial events. Identification of MIS 5a/b tidal deposits, whose uppermost parts are deeply oxidised, confirms that two cycles of sea-level fluctuation occurred prior to the final postglacial transgression. Thus, these high-frequency sequences are considered to be fifth-order subsequences (lower ss1 and upper ss2) with 40 kyr time intervals. 

• Most importantly, sequence boundaries tend to disappear in the relatively seaward position because of erosion by subsequent waves. As clearly shown in core C01, it is difficult to recognise the bounding surfaces without age dates. This is due to the lack of tangible changes 

in lithology, BI, and oxidised layers often missing. This implies that the preservation of the hierarchical organisation of stratigraphic bounding surfaces is mainly controlled by sea-level changes, and thus care must be taken when dealing with sequence analyses of the rock record commonly based on lithostratigraphy only. 

• This study highlights that the MIS 6–MIS 1 succession was mostly controlled by sea-level changes. Prevailing transgressive sequences of multi-sequential orders, corresponding to major interglacials (MIS 5e and MIS 1) and minor interstadials (MIS 5a/b and MIS 3), show that transgressive episodes are significantly better preserved than forced regressive or stillstand deposits. The reason for this is the absence of a major river system capable of supplying large amounts of sediments. This study showcases how multi-order, multiple transgressive deposition may be preserved in a supply-limited, tectonically stable tidal nearshore setting.
